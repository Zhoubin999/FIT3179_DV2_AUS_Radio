Australian radio in the digital age

Streaming and podcasts have grown fast. Has radio been replaced, or has it changed shape? This page follows who listens, how they listen, and what they choose to hear.

Jargon buster

Words used on this page, in plain English. Hover over any mark in a chart for its exact numbers.

Surveys and numbers

Weekly reach
The percentage of people who used something at least once in the past 7 days.
Share
Of all the time people spent listening to radio in a city, the percentage that went to one station.
Points
The simple difference between two percentages: 58% vs 50% is a gap of 8 points.
Aged 10+ / adults 18+
Who each survey asked. Infinite Dial and GfK include people aged 10 and over; ACMA asks only adults, so its numbers can differ.
Ratings survey
GfK measures radio listening in each capital city several times a year. Surveys 3 to 6 are four of the 2026 rounds.
Program type
What a station mostly broadcasts: music, talk & news, or sport.

Radio technology

DAB+
Digital radio broadcast from towers, as an alternative to AM/FM. Clearer sound and more stations, but you need a DAB+ radio and a nearby transmitter.
Transmitter site
A geographic location hosting one or more radio transmitters. A site may broadcast several radio services.
kW (kilowatt)
The power of a transmitter. A 50 kW tower can usually reach further than a 0.3 kW “infill” site, which fills gaps between buildings or hills. Terrain and antennas also matter.
Frequency block
A DAB+ channel such as 9A or 9C. Each block carries a bundle of several stations at once, so one transmitter can send many stations.
Relay transmitter
A small transmitter that repeats a station from somewhere else so a far-away town can hear it (officially, a “retransmission”).
01

The big shift: streaming caught up with radio

Streaming and podcast listening grew strongly while radio remained widely used.

Streaming overtook radio in 2025, but radio is still heard by 3 in 4 people

Since 2017, weekly streaming rose from 46% to 81% and podcasts from 10% to 45%, while radio slipped only from 85% to 77%. With 81% streaming and 77% on radio in 2026, at least about 58% of Australians used both each week: the survey shows overlap, not listeners switching.

Spotify leads YouTube Music by 18 points (47% vs 29%), and growth slowed in 2026

Spotify is used weekly by almost half of Australians (47%), and YouTube Music is a clear second at 29%, almost double Apple Music's 16%. Apple Music and Amazon Music grew fastest in 2025, then held steady in 2026. Three years is too short to say whether growth has stopped.

02

Who listens: age and location matter

Weekly radio reach is highest among older Australians. FM is used more outside the capitals, while DAB+, streaming and podcasts are used more in the cities.

Young regional Australians have the lowest weekly radio reach

8 in 10 Australians aged 55+ listen to radio each week, in cities and regions alike. Among 10–24 year olds the gap is 10 points: 70% nationally but only 60% in regional areas. This is a 2026 snapshot, so it compares groups rather than showing change over time.

A third of Australians aged 10+ listen to and watch podcasts

Podcast audio and video audiences overlap. In 2026, 55% of Australians aged 10+ consumed podcasts each month: 35% of all Australians both listened and watched, while only 5% watched without listening. Monthly podcast use is lower in regional Australia (47%).

Regional Australians stick with FM; city residents go digital

FM radio is 8 points more popular in regional areas (58% vs 50%), while DAB+ digital radio is 10 points more popular in the cities (14% vs 4%). Streaming and podcasts also lean towards the cities. Chapter 3 shows that DAB+ towers are concentrated in the capitals, which may help explain the gap.

Why 14% here but 42% in the first chart? This chart uses the ACMA survey of adults, while the first chart uses Infinite Dial, which asks people aged 10+ a different question. Compare numbers within one survey, not across them.

03

How Australians reach radio, and where digital radio can operate

Technology is changing both how people reach radio (in the car, at home, on smart speakers) and where digital broadcasting is available.

Radio lives in the car, and that is where phones now compete

The car is radio's stronghold: 67% of Australians heard radio in a vehicle in the past week, half as many again as at home (44%). But 52% of adults who travel by car now have Apple CarPlay or Android Auto, which puts streaming apps on the same dashboard. Regional listeners trail in every place, by up to 8 points at work, on smart speakers, at the gym and on public transport.

Regional homes are more likely to have a radio, but less likely to have a digital one

46 in 100 regional adults have a radio at home, compared with 42 in the capital cities, but only 22 in 100 have a DAB+ digital radio, compared with 28. The next map shows that the DAB+ sites in the ACMA records are concentrated in city regions, a possible contributor to this gap, though not proof of why households choose a receiver.

Digital radio is built for the big cities

The July 2026 ACMA licence records analysed here list 29 distinct DAB+ transmitter sites. Each capital has at least one high-power site (20–50 kW), and Sydney and Melbourne add several low-power “infill” sites. All 29 sites are in these 11 city regions, which may help explain why only 4% of regional adults use DAB+.

In the Australia view, ring size counts the sites in each state. Zoomed in, circle size shows licensed transmitter power in kilowatts (kW). Higher power can support wider coverage, but terrain and antennas also matter, so this is not a coverage map.

Radio infrastructure is not spread in proportion to population

The Northern Territory has 32.7 radio transmitter sites per 100,000 people, more than 12 times Victoria's 2.6. Dispersed settlements may help explain the pattern, but site counts alone cannot measure coverage or reception quality.

Across the five cities, the overall mix barely moved

On average across the five cities, commercial music slipped 1.4 points between Survey 3 and Survey 6 (54.2% to 52.8%), while the share not accounted for by individually listed stations rose 2.0 points (20.1% to 22.1%). Talk and news held steady at about 17%. This calm average hides bigger, opposite moves in individual cities: commercial music fell 4.8 points in Brisbane but rose 4.0 in Perth.

Each city counts equally in the average (not weighted by population or listening hours), so these are not national shares. Ribbons join each program type's share from one survey to the next; they do not show listeners moving between formats.

Music dominates all five cities, but talk and news and unrated listening distinguish them. In Survey 6, talk and news ranges from 13% in Brisbane to 22% in Melbourne, and Perth has the largest music share (64%).

Who climbed and who slipped in the top five

Across the five cities, the top of the ratings rarely changes: four kept the same No. 1 in all four surveys, and only Melbourne's is a talk station. Brisbane is the exception, where the top spot changed hands twice (Triple M, then B105, then 4BH 1116) with the leaders within 1.7 points. 05

What this means for Australian radio

Radio has not been replaced, but it is no longer the default. Its future depends on reaching people where they now listen.

77%

Still a weekly habit. Streaming overtook radio in 2025, but radio still reaches 77% of Australians aged 10+ each week, and 8 in 10 of those aged 55+.

60%

Young regional listeners are the hardest to reach. Only 60% of regional 10–24 year olds listen weekly, 10 points below the national figure for their age.

29

Digital radio is mainly a city service. All 29 DAB+ sites in the ACMA records are in 11 city regions, and regional adults use FM more than city adults.

22%

Local tastes still matter. Talk and news take 22% of total radio listening in Melbourne but 13% in Brisbane, so one national formula would not fit every city.

For listeners, radio remains free and local, especially in the car and outside the capitals. For broadcasters, keeping that audience means being on phones, car screens and smart speakers, and extending digital radio beyond the big cities.

How the data was combined. Every chart reads one file, australian_radio_data.csv. It merges the Infinite Dial and ACMA survey results, four GfK ratings surveys (with a program type for each station from an explicit table, station_program_types.csv), ACMA DAB+ transmitter sites, and ACMA radio sites joined to ABS state populations. A dataset column says which kind each row is. The original downloads are in data/raw on GitHub and the merge script is data/build_merged_data.py. Surveys cover different ages (ACMA: adults 18+; Infinite Dial and GfK: people 10+), so their numbers are shown in separate charts rather than mixed together.
